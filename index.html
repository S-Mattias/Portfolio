Assignment 1: Process

1. Design Premises and Inspiration

I started by looking at templates online and chose the "Dimension" template as inspiration because it is very sleek and isolates content in pop-up windows. I decided to make the website more engaging by using a black hole video I created a while ago as the background. The color palette (black, white, and gray) was chosen to fit the "Space/Sci-Fi" theme that the video gives off.

2. Media Processing and Technical Implementation

Video Processing: The original black hole video was a large file. To ensure the website loads quickly without freezing, I had to use video editing software to compress the video and save it as an .mp4 file. I then placed a dark, semi-transparent layer (CSS overlay) over the video to ensure the white text is always easily readable.

Programming: I built the site using HTML5 and CSS3, and used CSS Flexbox to ensure the windows and menu are always centered, regardless of screen size.

3. Github Deployment

To upload the site to the web, I used Git Bash (command line). I went through the following steps:

  • Used git init to create a local repository.
  • Used git add . and git commit -m "Initial setup" to save changes.
  • Connected the folder to Github using git remote add origin and finally uploaded using git push.

It was a slight challenge making sure all paths to the video and documents were correct so they worked after the site went live, but that was solved by having a well-organized assets folder.

Assignment 2: Computer-Aided Manufacturing

1. Introduction and Concept

The goal of this assignment was to explore computer-aided manufacturing through both vinyl cutting and laser cutting. I designed and manufactured the Samaso logo as a sticker to put on my laptop.

2. Vinyl Cutting

Process: I started by designing the vector graphic in [Inkscape / Illustrator]. The cutting area was kept within the 100x50cm limit. I exported the file to the vinyl cutter software, adjusted the blade settings, and cut out the design.

Execution: After the cut, I removed the excess vinyl (weeding) and used transfer tape to apply the sticker smoothly onto my laptop.

Figure 1: The final applied vinyl cut design.

3. Parametric Design and Press-Fit

Fusion 360 Design: I used Autodesk Fusion 360 to create the 3D model. The requirement was to make the model fully parametric, meaning I set up user parameters such as 3mm, width, and height. By changing these numbers, the entire model scales automatically without breaking.

Assembly Features: The design incorporates [NUMBER] press-fit joints. I utilized [NAME THE JOINT TYPES, e.g., finger joints and slot joints] to ensure the pieces fit perfectly together without the need for glue.

Figure 2: Screenshot from Fusion 360 showcasing the parameters and the 3D model.

4. Group Project: Kerf Testing

It was just me for this project, I used the laser cutter to measure the "kerf" (the amount of material burned away by the laser beam).

Testing: We cut a test comb with slots of incrementally decreasing sizes to find the perfect friction fit for 3mm acrylic. Our conclusion was that the kerf for this specific machine and material was 0,2mm. I then input this exact number as a parameter in my Fusion 360 model to compensate for the laser beam thickness.

Figure 3: Our group's kerf test used to determine the exact material tolerances.

5. Cutting, Assembly, and Final Product

I exported the flat faces of my design as DXF files from Fusion 360 and brought them into [Inkscape / Illustrator] to ensure all lines were formatted correctly for the laser cutter (e.g., setting line colors/weights for cutting vs. engraving). The model was then cut out using the laser cutter.

The pieces were collected and assembled. Because of the kerf compensation, the press-fit joints worked flawlessly, resulting in a very sturdy final product.

Figure 4: The fully assembled press-fit product.

Assignment 3: 3D Printing and Scanning

1. Ideation, Keywords, and Research

The goal of this assignment was to design and print an object that specifically requires 3D printing (additive manufacturing) and to test the limits of the printer. To find inspiration and research the possibilities, I used the following search keywords on Google and YouTube: "Print in place mechanisms", "impossible shapes subtractive manufacturing", "3D printing tolerance test", and "Prusa MK3+ design constraints". I learned a lot by studying how others have designed moving parts that print fully assembled.

2. The Design: Additive vs. Subtractive

I designed a foot for the drone I am building for Tölvustýrður vélbúnaður using Autodesk Fusion 360.

[Image of additive vs subtractive manufacturing]

Why 3D printing? This design would be impossible to manufacture using subtractive methods, such as CNC milling. The reason is that the model contains [internal cavities / moving parts that print together / complex undercuts] where a milling tool could never reach to remove the material.

3. 3D Printer Testing and Design Rules

Before printing the final model, I had to determine the design rules and constraints of the printer (Prusa MK3+). I designed and printed a tolerance and overhang test piece that included different gaps between parts (ranging from 0.1mm to 0.5mm) and various overhang angles.

Test Results: I discovered that the printer can successfully handle a gap without the parts fusing together, and it can print up to a angle without needing support material. These test results directly influenced my final design; I went back into Fusion 360 and adjusted the clearances on my moving parts to exactly to ensure perfect functionality.

Figure 1: Tolerance and overhang test prints.

4. Final Product and Learning Outcome

The final model was printed on a Prusa MK3+. The slicing software (PrusaSlicer) indicated that the object would use of plastic, which is well under the 100g limit. The model printed successfully and functioned exactly as intended.

What I learned: I learned how crucial it is to understand the tolerances of each specific machine. My initial CAD sketch had clearances that were too tight and would have fused together if I hadn't conducted those basic tests first.

Figure 2: The completed 3D printed model.
